## Part 1: Stress Management

Stress management matters to me because I deal with stress often, and I have struggled with it for a long time without fully understanding why, whether it comes from family expectations, schoolwork, or just everyday pressure. Baldwin et al. (2020) explain that stress becomes damaging once it turns chronic, affecting concentration, sleep, and physical health, which matches what happens to me when stress builds up instead of being addressed early.

One practice I already rely on is talking to my husband and my dad when something is bothering me. Saying the problem out loud and having them listen has worked reasonably well for me in the past, even if the relief does not always last very long. Music, on the other hand, has never done much for me, which tells me that stress relief is not one-size-fits-all; a method that works for someone else does not necessarily work for me.

The first thing I want to improve is my sleep schedule, since it has been inconsistent for a while and makes everything else harder to manage. Once that is more stable, the new practice I plan to adopt is yoga or meditation, something I have never done consistently before. Baldwin et al. (2020) note that sitting quietly for even a few minutes can lower heart rate and blood pressure, which gives me hope that a regular practice like this could build real **resilience** against daily stress over time. To determine whether it is working, I plan to notice how rested and calm I feel from week to week rather than expecting quick **progress**, and to hold onto some **optimism** even if early results are small.

## Part 2: Test Anxiety

While preparing for my final exam, I intend to stay calm by talking with the people closest to me about how I am feeling, since that has already proven effective for me. I also plan to use my notes and set specific study times instead of studying whenever I happen to have time, which requires a level of **self-regulation** I am still working on. A small amount of stress during the exam itself can even help maintain focus instead of being something to eliminate entirely (Baldwin et al., 2020), so my goal is to manage the nervousness rather than expect it to disappear.

On exam day itself, I do not plan to touch any reading materials at all, since I want my mind to stay fresh instead of cramming, which has only made me more anxious in the past. This lines up with the textbook's advice to arrive early and calm the mind before the exam begins rather than absorbing new material right beforehand (Baldwin et al., 2020). Once the exam starts, I plan to read the instructions carefully, answer the questions I feel most confident about first, and mark harder ones to return to later.

I chose these strategies because they combine what has already worked for me, like relying on my husband and dad, with techniques the reading recommends, such as timed studying and arriving mentally prepared rather than overloaded. Sticking with this routine takes real **persistence**, especially on days when motivation is low, but consistency will matter more than any single method on its own.
