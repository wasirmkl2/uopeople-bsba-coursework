# Discussion Forum Unit 1: Pre-Writing Strategies and the Scientific Method

**Posted by:** Saira Binte Shahriar
**Course:** ENGL 1102-01 — English Composition 2 (AY2027-T1)

Cooking is the thing that excites me more than almost anything else, and someday I want to take that further and open a restaurant of my own. Since this assignment asked me to explore a topic academically, that felt like an easy choice. Before writing anything, I opened the Notes app on my phone and started listing whatever came to mind: culinary school, learning on the job, knife skills, plating, "how do chefs really get good?" In Hall and Wallace's (2018) terms, this kind of unfiltered listing is brainstorming, and it works precisely because it separates generating ideas from judging them (Section 4.1). Once I had the list down, I noticed most of it circled back to one question I keep wondering about: whether going to culinary school really teaches someone to cook better than just working their way up in a real kitchen would.

Serdikoff (2021) explains that science starts with turning a general curiosity into something specific enough to test, and that's basically what happened here. My research question became this: does formal culinary school training build stronger real-world kitchen skills than learning mainly through hands-on kitchen work? What I'm proposing is that hands-on experience develops speed and practical skill faster, since a busy kitchen forces someone to adapt immediately, while culinary school probably covers technique and food science in more depth but at a slower, more structured pace.

Looking into this wouldn't need a lab, just real information. I'd look up interviews and career profiles of working chefs, some who trained formally and some who came up through kitchens without a culinary degree, and compare what each group says helped them most early on. Specific things worth watching for: how quickly each person says they picked up plating and timing, any knowledge gaps they mention the other path ended up filling, and how they describe handling pressure once service gets busy. Serdikoff (2021) points to comparisons like this as one way research replaces guesswork with real evidence, which is precisely the shift this whole process gave me.

Between prewriting and the scientific method, a loose dream about owning a restaurant turned into a real question I could dig into properly. The brainstorming list gave me the raw pieces, but figuring out how to test the idea is what turned it into something worth researching rather than something I only think about. Since I plan to run a kitchen myself one day, understanding which path builds the skills I'll need matters to me well beyond just this one assignment.
