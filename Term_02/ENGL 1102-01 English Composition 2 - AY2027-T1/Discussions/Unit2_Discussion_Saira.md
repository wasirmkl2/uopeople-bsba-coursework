# Discussion Forum Unit 2: Narrative Mode — Opening My Own Restaurant, One Day

**Posted by:** Saira Binte Shahriar
**Course:** ENGL 1102-01 — English Composition 2 (AY2027-T1)

Instead of arguing a position or explaining how something works, the narrative mode is all about telling a story, whether it's something that really happened or something you're imagining. That's basically what Mallika (2024) means by narration: storytelling that lays out a sequence of events, usually in some kind of order, built around characters, conflict, and an arc, not just a pile of facts. The five paragraphs that follow also stick to the basic shape Hall and Wallace (2018) lay out for an essay: an opening that sets the story up, body paragraphs that develop it, and a conclusion that brings it together. Since I haven't actually opened a restaurant, this narrative imagines the process, which the assignment allows, following the concept from its first spark through the one obstacle that nearly derailed it.

The whole thing started on an ordinary evening, somewhere between finishing a bowl of noodles and wondering why nowhere nearby served food with the depth of real Thai and Pan-Asian cooking. I wanted a place people could sink into, low lighting, warm colors, the kind of cozy corner booth you don't want to leave, paired with food bold enough to match the atmosphere. The concept kept expanding every time I pictured it: a multi-cuisine menu rooted in those same flavors, and a sauce bar where every table gets unlimited sauces to pair however they like. That last detail became the whole personality of the place in my head before I had even written a single line of a business plan.

Turning that vision into something real meant testing recipes, comparing suppliers, and figuring out what the space itself needed to feel the way I imagined it. Weeks went into narrowing down a menu that stayed true to those cuisines without becoming so broad it lost its identity. I sketched the layout more times than I can count, chasing that exact cozy feeling: soft seating, dim warm lighting, small tables close enough to feel intimate but not cramped. Every choice, from the paint color to the size of the sauce station, was really one long argument I was having with myself about what "cozy" was supposed to look like on a plate and in a room.

Then came the setback that nearly stopped everything. The specific ingredients the menu depended on, fresh galangal, particular chili varieties, a certain fish sauce brand, weren't something a local supplier could reliably provide, and the one distributor who could import them wanted a minimum order far bigger than a new restaurant could justify. For a while, the entire concept risked turning into generic Asian fusion instead of the specific, authentic flavors the whole idea had been built around. I spent longer than expected calling around, comparing smaller specialty importers, before finding one willing to work with a lower minimum in exchange for a standing weekly order. It wasn't the deal I originally wanted, but it kept the menu exactly what it needed to be.

Even just imagining the process this closely, the lesson comes through clearly: the vision survives by adapting, not by refusing to bend. The cozy atmosphere and the unlimited sauces stayed exactly as planned, but getting there meant compromising on how the ingredients arrived, not on what the food actually tasted like. If I ever open this restaurant for real, that's the part I'd carry in from the very first table: an idea only stays intact once you're willing to change how you get there.
