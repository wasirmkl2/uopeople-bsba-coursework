# Discussion Forum Unit 2 — BUS 1102-01

**How Transactions Affect Accounts and Financial Statements**
Saira Binte Shahriar

ABC Corp's June transactions look pretty different from each other on the surface, but every one of them still answers to the same rule: Assets = Liabilities + Equity has to stay balanced no matter what. Liabilities and equity might both sit on the same side of that equation, but they represent very different kinds of obligations underneath.

**Liability vs. Equity: Impact on Financial Position**

Take the $2,500 advance payment ABC Corp received on June 12. The cash itself is still an asset the company now owns, but it comes paired with a liability, unearned revenue, because ABC Corp hasn't delivered the office supplies yet and still owes that order. Compare that to the $8,000 the owner put into the business on June 20 — equity increases instead, since the owner is contributing personal funds with no expectation of getting repaid. Assets go up by a similar amount in both cases, but the right side of the balance sheet moves in opposite directions, and the equation still balances either way. According to Franklin et al. (2022), the accounting equation has to stay balanced no matter which side changes, though the type of change still matters to whoever is reading the statements afterward. A liability represents an obligation the company still has to fulfill, such as delivering the goods in the case of unearned revenue, while equity represents the owner's claim on the business with no repayment attached. For ABC Corp, the $8,000 owner contribution increases equity without creating a repayment obligation, unlike a liability.

**Recording the Advance Payment**

The $2,500 can't be booked as revenue yet, since the office supplies aren't going out the door until July. Per the revenue recognition principle, revenue only gets recorded once it's actually earned, not just whenever cash happens to show up (Franklin et al., 2022). So the journal entry for June 12 looks like this:

| Date | Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| June 12, 2024 | Cash | $2,500 | |
| June 12, 2024 | Unearned Revenue | | $2,500 |

Keeping it as a liability is what keeps June's income statement honest. Once ABC Corp delivers the supplies in July, it can debit Unearned Revenue and credit Sales Revenue for $2,500, reducing the liability and recognizing the revenue in the period it was actually earned. If that step got skipped, June would end up looking more profitable than it really was, and July would take the hit instead.

**Timing of Expense Recognition**

Both the $1,200 in wages on June 15 and the $500 utility bill on June 25 happened and got paid in June, which means June's statements show what it actually cost to keep the business running that month. Franklin et al. (2022) call this the expense recognition principle, or the matching principle — the idea that expenses belong in the same period as the revenue they helped generate. Had ABC Corp pushed either expense into July instead, June's net income would come out looking better than it should, since the costs of operating that month wouldn't show up until later. Beyond just skewing one month's numbers, that kind of mismatch makes it harder to compare performance from month to month, and it could leave management or investors thinking June was more profitable than it actually was, right before an unexplained expense spike shows up in July that has nothing to do with July's own activity.
