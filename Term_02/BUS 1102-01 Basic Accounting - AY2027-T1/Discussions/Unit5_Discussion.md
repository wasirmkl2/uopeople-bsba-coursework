**Credit Recommendation**

My answer is no, at least not on Alpha Tech's terms. The current ratio of 1.33 means $1.33 in current assets per $1 of current liabilities, and a higher ratio makes covering short-term debt more likely (Franklin et al., 2022, Appendix A). Alpha Tech covers its current debts, but barely. The quick ratio bothers me more. It leaves out inventory and prepaid expenses because they can be slow to turn into cash (Franklin et al., 2022, Appendix A), so 0.33 means roughly a quarter of its current assets are readily available. A 5.0% profit margin means five cents of each sales dollar becomes profit, which is thin if volatility returns (Franklin et al., 2022, Appendix A). That is the kind of risk our upfront-payment rule for new clients covers, and in our busiest season I'd protect liquidity over winning the contract.

**Impact of a 30-Day Credit Period**

Picture November. We would be buying ingredients and paying staff for a $50,000 event, then waiting until December 30 to be paid. Our $120,000 in cash covers about 1.3 months of the $90,000 in monthly expenses, so the receivable ties up over half a month of costs. Reordering gets harder. Smaller lots could mean lost bulk pricing, and running short on inventory means lost sales (Franklin et al., 2022, Section 10.5).

Does tight cash change the valuation method? Not directly, since the method mostly affects reported profit and taxes. When prices rise, FIFO shows lower cost of goods sold and higher profit, while LIFO lowers taxable income (Franklin et al., 2022, Section 10.1). LIFO can look tempting in a cash crunch, but it isn't allowed on a tax return unless the financial statements use it too. Weighted average needs a new average after every inventory change, which the authors call laborious (Franklin et al., 2022, Section 10.1). I'd stay with FIFO. Its higher profit isn't cash, though, because holiday ingredients get replaced at current prices.

**Alternative Arrangement**

Here's what I'd offer instead: $25,000 at signing and the other $25,000 due December 15, 15 days after the event, our usual limit. The deposit helps cover food costs and stays unearned revenue until we cater the event, since revenue is recognized only once earned (Franklin et al., 2022, Section 9.1). CPA Australia (2020) backs this, recommending credit checks on new customers, deposits for big-ticket services, and terms the customer acknowledges. Credit still goes to Alpha Tech, and our exposure drops by half. Overly tight terms can push qualified customers toward competitors, while slow collection keeps cash tied up (Franklin et al., 2022, Section 9.3). A split handles both.

**If Alpha Tech Cannot Pay**

Say December 30 comes and nothing arrives. Cash of $120,000 plus $80,000 in other receivables is at most $200,000, and only if those customers pay. Two months of expenses run about $180,000, so the cushion shrinks to roughly $20,000. Alpha Tech's unpaid $50,000 would be 38% of our $130,000 in receivables. With the event's costs already spent and just $40,000 of inventory, less than half a month's expenses, reorders for later parties would compete with other bills. Most likely we'd delay purchases or turn down events. A late receivable lowers receivables turnover, a sign of slower collection (Franklin et al., 2022, Section 9.3). If it proves uncollectible, we'd write it off against the allowance for doubtful accounts, and any shortfall would add bad debt expense and reduce net income (Franklin et al., 2022, Section 9.2).
